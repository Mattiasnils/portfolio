Atlas Copco Industrial Technique

Background

Atlas Copco Industrial Technique (ITBA) builds industrial assembly solutions used in plants worldwide — tools, controllers, configuration software, and tightening analysis.

At its core, the work is about tightenings: setting up programs, running them on the line, giving operators clear feedback, and tracking quality. That spans people on the plant floor as well as Customer Centers supporting sites globally.

Px2 — Controller software for Power Focus 8

Background

Px2 is the controller software that runs on Power Focus 8 and IxB tools. It’s where technicians configure tightenings and where operators get feedback on the result.

Customer-specific result & feedback experience
Background

A large international car manufacturer asked for custom functionality across both the controller and tool result views — the feedback operators get when they tighten — in order to renew their frame agreement.

I was part of the early discussions with our local Customer Center (service engineers and sales). It didn’t take long to realize we needed more intimate research directly with the customer. So I took the initiative to arrange a factory visit: walking the line, interviewing people on site, and co-designing with interactive prototypes in a workshop.

That let us build detailed flows and concepts around their specific needs. We kept iterating with the Customer Center before implementation. Because the research was thorough, the functionality fit — and that contributed to a new frame agreement worth €1B+.

SyncMode migration & redesign
Background

I also migrated and redesigned SyncMode from ToolsTalk over to PF8 — to support extended functionality while improving the experience.

SyncMode is how technicians set up concurrent program execution in fixtured tool systems, including termination and repair paths, using a drag-and-drop node interface.

I researched the feature with internal Tightening Technicians, then built iterative prototypes and refined them with feedback until the interface could carry the added complexity without becoming harder to use.

Challenge: This is the most complex feature I’ve ever worked on. The domain is inherently hard — and the technical constraints and design language weren’t really made for this kind of functionality.

XB — Cordless tools for everyday assembly

Background

The idea behind XB was an affordable cordless tool family for C-class (customer-critical) and B-class (quality-critical) joints — as opposed to the more expensive IxB family, which is mainly aimed at A-class (safety-critical) joints and more advanced assembly needs.

XB also connects customers into the PF8 ecosystem: result tracking, centralized configuration, and tightening analysis. The tools run on their own platform and can connect wirelessly to Px2.

  • XCB — clutch tool for standard applications.
  • XTB — transducerized tool for standard and quality-critical applications, with torque and angle control and multiple programs.

Role & surfaces

I was the sole UX designer for both the configuration UI and the tool UI.

The tool display is a small 128×128 px OLED — black or white only, no greyscale — with three tactile buttons for navigation. The configuration UI is web-based: technicians set preferences and programs, and can view tool information and events.

Challenges

XB sits on a separate platform from PF8/IxB, with its own constraints and less compute power. I had to balance what the product needed on its own against staying coherent with the wider ecosystem. Ease of use mattered a lot too — unlike the more advanced Px2, XB should feel ready out of the box, without deep prior knowledge of tightening technique.

And then there’s the display itself: 128×128 pixels on a tiny monochrome screen.

I worked closely with the Px2 design team, and proactively with the XB product owner, product manager, and developers, to adapt parts of Px2 functionality onto this more concentrated platform.

Outcome

The complete XB tool family was released on 26 March 2026. Early customer and industry feedback has focused on out-of-the-box ease of use, operator comfort, and line efficiency. From a design standpoint the reception has been highly favorable — notably earning the 2026 Red Dot Design Award for its focus on modern manufacturing ergonomics.

Design system — Coherence across ITBA

Background

Alongside product work, I also spent part of my time on a grassroots design system effort — without a dedicated team at the start. The goal was a more systematic way of designing across ITBA: reusable, scalable, and coherent.

It grew into a collaboration across roughly 15 UX designers, with different people owning north star, style, tokens, components, patterns, and information architecture. I was involved in several of those areas, but mainly responsible for token architecture and component design plus documentation.

The scope covers all ITBA products — about a dozen web and mobile apps, and four tool applications.

Process

For each component, the loop looked like this:

  • Audit how the component showed up across products
  • Look at best practices in established design systems
  • Assemble requirements
  • Design the component and document usage guidelines
  • Review and implement with two development teams using React, Radix, and Tailwind
Outcome

The result is a library of 40+ base components (buttons, inputs, tables, lists, checkboxes, and so on) built on tokens, documented in Figma, and implemented in code. We’re still rolling them into products — they aren’t used in shipping UIs yet, but that’s coming soon.

Working across the ecosystem

What made this role different from a single product case was working on all three at once — switching between controller software, a constrained tool platform, and shared design foundations.

Collaborating with the Px2 design team helped XB stay coherent with the ecosystem without copying PF8 wholesale. We adapted concepts to a simpler platform and a more approachable mental model.

The product work also fed the design system. Auditing real apps shaped tokens and components — and as the library lands in products, that shared baseline goes back into the next features.
